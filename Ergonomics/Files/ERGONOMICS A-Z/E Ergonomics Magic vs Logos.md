    - PROLEGOMENA: THE FORGOTTEN QUESTION
    - What makes a word mean what it means? This question, which once occupied the brightest minds of Athens and Alexandria, has been systematically expelled from modern intellectual discourse. Contemporary linguistics treats it as naive; philosophy of language buries it under technical apparatus; cognitive science dissolves it into neural correlates. Yet the question refuses to disappear because it is not ultimately about words but about reality itself—whether reality exists independent of human naming, and whether human speech can participate in or only impose upon that reality.
    - The erasure of this question is not accidental. It serves identifiable interests. A civilization that cannot ask what makes words true cannot detect when words have been corrupted. A population trained to treat meaning as arbitrary convention cannot recognise when their vocabulary has been captured. A scholarly class that dismisses etymology as "the etymological fallacy" cannot trace how fundamental concepts have been inverted while their surface forms remain.
    - This dissertation proposes that there exist precisely two fundamental principles governing the meaning of words—not as competing theories among many, but as exhaustive alternatives representing the deepest choice available to conscious beings regarding their relationship to reality. These principles are:
    - The Principle of Magic: Words derive meaning from mental operations. The human mind (individual or collective) generates meaning through acts of will, convention, construction, or projection. Reality as such either does not exist, or exists but is inaccessible except through linguistic construction. Words are therefore essentially arbitrary placeholders whose "meaning" reduces to patterns of use within language games. The logical endpoint: if meaning is manufactured, then controlling the manufacture controls thought itself.
    - The Principle of Logos (God or Nature): Words derive meaning from participation in an order that transcends human construction. Reality exists objectively; it exhibits rational structure (λόγος) that precedes and enables human language. Words, properly formed, function as vessels that receive and transmit aspects of this objective order. Etymology matters because good words were crafted to preserve essential recognitions. The logical endpoint: if meaning participates in objective truth, then corrupting words corrupts the soul's access to reality.
    - The choice between these principles determines everything downstream: epistemology, ethics, politics, pedagogy, law, science, theology. It is the meta-choice that shapes all other choices. And modern civilization has chosen—or rather, been led to choose—the Principle of Magic while being told it has chosen scientific rationality. Examining how this happened, and what it means, constitutes the central task of this investigation.

    - PART ONE: THE ANATOMY OF THE MAGICAL PRINCIPLE
    - Chapter One: The Invisible Confession of Modern Semantics
    - The Principle of Magic governs modern linguistic and cognitive science under various technical names that conceal its nature: conventionalism, constructivism, structuralism, formalism, pragmatism. Each variant shares the core commitment: meaning originates in human operations rather than correspondence to mind-independent reality. Yet this commitment, when pressed, produces strange confessions.
    - Ferdinand de Saussure, whose Cours de linguistique générale (1916) founded modern structural linguistics, articulated the principle with unusual clarity:
    - "The bond between the signifier and the signified is arbitrary... the idea of 'sister' is not linked by any inner relationship to the succession of sounds s-ö-r which serves as its signifier in French; it could be represented equally by just any other sequence."¹
    - This appears as descriptive linguistics, merely noting that different languages use different sounds for the same concept. But observe the metaphysical commitment smuggled in: the "idea" of sister is treated as prior to and independent of any linguistic expression, yet this idea has no necessary connection to any particular signifier. Where, then, does the idea reside? What ensures that my "idea" of sister corresponds to yours? Saussure's answer—convention, agreement, the language system itself—merely relocates the question without answering it.
    - The structural solution generates new problems. If meaning is determined entirely within the language system through differences and oppositions, then language becomes what Saussure called "a form and not a substance." But forms of what? The structuralist account cannot specify what the forms are forms of without reintroducing the correspondence to reality it officially denies. Ludwig Wittgenstein confronted this impasse directly:
    - "For a large class of cases—though not for all—in which we employ the word 'meaning' it can be defined thus: the meaning of a word is its use in the language."²
    - Notice the hedging: "for a large class of cases—though not for all." Even Wittgenstein, who built an entire philosophical edifice on language games and use, could not eliminate the residual cases where meaning seems to be more than use. What are these cases? He never specified, because specifying them would have reopened the question he sought to close.
    - The American pragmatist tradition proved equally evasive. William James wrote:
    - "True ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify. False ideas are those that we cannot... The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events."³
    - Here the Principle of Magic achieves explicit formulation: truth is made, not discovered. Ideas do not correspond to reality; they are manufactured into truth through operations of validation. But who validates? By what criteria? James's pragmatism covertly imports standards (assimilation, verification) that it cannot ground within its own framework. The validators must already know what counts as validation—which presupposes the correspondence theory supposedly being replaced.
    - Chapter Two: The Operational Magic in Scientific Vocabulary
    - Consider how contemporary science actually operates with vocabulary. The physicist speaks of "forces," "fields," "particles," "waves"—words inherited from ordinary language, pressed into technical service, then reified as descriptions of fundamental reality. But when pressed, the physicist retreats: "These are just models. We don't know what reality really is at the quantum level. Our mathematics works; interpretation is philosophy."
    - Richard Feynman captured this operational agnosticism with characteristic directness:
    - "It is important to realize that in physics today, we have no knowledge of what energy is. We do not have a picture that energy comes in little blobs of a definite amount... There are formulas for calculating some numerical quantity, and when we add it all together it gives '28'—always the same number."⁴
    - This is the Principle of Magic operating at the foundations of physics. "Energy" functions as a placeholder for whatever the mathematics calculates. The word means its use in equations. No correspondence to objective reality is claimed—or rather, the question is systematically evaded by labeling it "philosophical."
    - Yet observe what happens in scientific popularisation and cultural transmission. The same physicists who disclaim metaphysical knowledge within technical discourse confidently pronounce on ultimate reality in public. The "random" mutations of evolutionary theory—operationally meaning "not predictable by current models"—become in cultural transmission "purposeless," "blind," "without design." The operational humility vanishes; the magical word "random" is treated as though it described reality's ultimate nature.
    - This is vocabulary operating under what we might call cryptic magical realism: officially denying that words correspond to reality while practically treating specific word-choices as though they revealed reality's nature. The word "random" does not merely name human ignorance; it is deployed to deny that reality exhibits rational order. The word "natural" in "natural selection" does not merely mark contrast with artificial breeding; it is deployed to deny that selection exhibits intelligence or purpose.
    - The MESS pattern emerges: two incompatible claims held simultaneously. Technically, words are arbitrary placeholders whose "meaning" is operational. Culturally, specific vocabulary choices carry massive metaphysical freight, shaping how entire populations understand their place in the cosmos. The framework gains authority from technical humility while wielding cultural power through metaphysical assertion.
    - Chapter Three: The Self-Refutation of Radical Constructivism
    - Push the Principle of Magic to its limit and watch it consume itself. If all meaning is constructed, then the statement "all meaning is constructed" is itself constructed. If language cannot access mind-independent reality, then the claim "language cannot access mind-independent reality" cannot access mind-independent reality either. If truth is merely consensus or power-effect, then the claim "truth is merely consensus or power-effect" is itself merely consensus or power-effect.
    - The postmodern current made this self-refutation explicit through what Richard Rorty called "final vocabularies":
    - "All human beings carry about a set of words which they employ to justify their actions, their beliefs, and their lives... I shall call these words a person's 'final vocabulary'... It is 'final' in the sense that if doubt is cast on the worth of these words, their user has no noncircular argumentative recourse."⁵
    - Observe the confession: we all operate with vocabularies whose meaning we cannot ultimately justify. Our most fundamental words are groundless. This is the Principle of Magic acknowledging its own foundation in void—meaning without ground, words without world.
    - But Rorty continued teaching, writing, arguing. He employed words as though they meant something, persuaded audiences as though persuasion mattered, criticised alternatives as though criticism had standards. The performative contradiction was total. His students were trained to believe that meaning is groundless while being expected to understand their teacher's meaning. They wrote dissertations demonstrating that all vocabularies are equally arbitrary—and expected those dissertations to be evaluated by standards that could distinguish better from worse arguments.
    - Jacques Derrida pushed further with his doctrine that meaning is always "deferred" through différance:
    - "The play of differences supposes, in effect, syntheses and referrals which forbid at any moment, or in any sense, that a simple element be present in and of itself, referring only to itself."⁶
    - Nothing is present to itself; meaning is endlessly deferred through chains of signifiers. Yet Derrida's own writing—notoriously difficult, densely allusive, requiring expert interpretation—demanded that readers understand what he meant, recognise his arguments, grasp his distinctions. The more insistently he denied presence and meaning, the more his texts required sophisticated hermeneutical presence to extract their meaning.
    - This is not mere inconsistency but structural necessity. The Principle of Magic, fully articulated, annihilates the conditions for its own articulation. One cannot state that language fails to represent reality without making a representational claim about language and reality. One cannot argue that arguments are merely power moves without expecting the argument to be assessed as something other than a power move.

    - PART TWO: THE RECOVERY OF THE LOGOCENTRIC PRINCIPLE
    - Chapter Five: The Johannine Foundation
    - The Principle of Logos receives its most concentrated expression in the Johannine prologue:
    - Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος. οὗτος ἦν ἐν ἀρχῇ πρὸς τὸν θεόν. πάντα δι᾽ αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο, καὶ χωρὶς αὐτοῦ ἐγένετο οὐδὲ ἕν ὃ γέγονεν.
    - "In the beginning was the Logos, and the Logos was with God, and the Logos was God. He was in the beginning with God. All things came into being through him, and without him not one thing came into being."⁷
    - The Greek λόγος cannot be translated by any single English term because it names a complex of meanings that modern vocabulary has fractured: word, reason, account, ratio, proportion, order, principle. The Stoics used λόγος for the rational principle governing the cosmos. Heraclitus named it the eternal ordering that most humans fail to perceive. Philo of Alexandria identified it as the mediating principle between transcendent God and created world.
    - John's prologue appropriates this entire tradition while transforming it: the Logos is not merely rational principle but personal—"with God" and "was God" simultaneously, distinct yet identical. And crucially, the Logos is creative: "all things came into being through him." The world is not merely described by rational order; it is generated through Logos. Reality at its foundation is linguistic in the deepest sense—not as human construction but as divine articulation.
    - This theological claim carries immediate semantic implications. If reality is generated through Logos, then reality has linguistic structure built into its constitution. Words that correspond to this structure participate in Logos; words that contradict it oppose Logos. The distinction between true and false naming becomes ontologically grounded—not in convention but in the rational order through which all things came into being.
    - Consider the difference from the magical principle. Under magic, human language creates meaning ex nihilo or through social agreement. Under Logos, human language discovers meaning that precedes and enables human speech. The craftsman of words does not invent meaning but discerns it—as Plato's Socrates says in the Cratylus, the name-maker must know "the natural name suited to each thing" (390d).
    - Chapter Six: Adam's Naming as Rational Participation
    - The Genesis account of Adam naming the animals (Genesis 2:19-20) provides the paradigmatic instance of Logocentric naming:
    - "And out of the ground the LORD God formed every beast of the field and every bird of the sky and brought them to Adam to see what he would call them. And whatever Adam called a living creature, that was its name."
    - Notice the structure: God creates, then brings creatures to Adam "to see what he would call them." The naming is neither arbitrary nor predetermined. God observes what Adam will name each creature—suggesting genuine creaturely participation in the naming process. Yet "whatever Adam called a living creature, that was its name"—suggesting that Adam's naming achieves real reference, not merely provisional labeling.
    - Philo of Alexandria interpreted this passage as demonstrating Adam's rational participation in divine Logos:
    - "The first man's mind was accurate, without alloy of other considerations, and he alone can be said to have named every creature by the best name, for his soul was fashioned by the Logos of the Maker of all... He observed each of its powers and gave it the name fitting to its nature."⁸
    - For Philo, Adam named correctly because his prelapsarian intellect participated in the same Logos through which creatures were made. Names corresponded to natures because both emerged from the same rational source. The Fall corrupted this correspondence—hence the multiplication of languages, the confusion of terms, the possibility of naming amiss.
    - The Kabbalistic tradition developed this further, teaching that Hebrew letters carry divine creative power (צירופי אותיות), that the Name of God sustains existence, that proper naming participates in ontological creativity. Whether or not one accepts the mystical framework, the structural insight stands: under the Logocentric principle, words are not arbitrary signs but participations in the rational order of being.
    - Contrast this with the magical principle's implicit anthropology. If words create meaning through human will or convention, then Adam's naming becomes either entirely arbitrary (any word serves equally well) or entirely determined (God tells Adam what to say). The Genesis account suggests neither: Adam genuinely names while his naming genuinely succeeds. This middle position requires the Logocentric assumption that human reason participates in the same order that structures reality.
    - Chapter Seven: Plato's Cratylus and the Natural Correctness of Names
    - Plato's Cratylus remains the foundational text in Western philosophy of language precisely because it refuses to choose between pure naturalism and pure conventionalism while exposing the inadequacies of both. Hermogenes defends convention: "Whatever name you give, in my opinion, is the right one, and if you change that and give another, the new name is as correct as the old—for there is no name given to anything by nature; all is convention and habit of the users."⁹ Cratylus defends nature: names belong naturally to their specific objects; if you try to speak of something with any name other than its natural name, you are simply failing to refer to it at all.
    - Socrates navigates between them with characteristic irony. Against pure conventionalism, he argues that naming is a skilled activity (τέχνη) like weaving or drilling—the craftsman must attend to the nature of what he works with. A shuttle must be suited to its function of separating warp and weft; a name must be suited to its function of "teaching" (διδάσκειν) and "distinguishing essences" (διακρίνειν τὴν οὐσίαν).¹⁰ This grounds naming in function rather than arbitrary choice.
    - But against Cratylus's extreme naturalism, Socrates demonstrates that actual Greek names contain imperfections—sounds that don't fit their alleged natural meanings, foreign corruptions, historical mutations. Perfect correspondence between name and nature is unachievable in practice, though it remains the ideal toward which good naming aims.
    - The dialogue's resolution lies in Socrates's portrait of the ideal name-maker (ὀνοματουργός):
    - "So mustn't a rule-setter also know how to embody in sounds and syllables the name naturally suited to each thing? And if he is to be an authentic giver of names, mustn't he, in making and giving each name, look to what a name itself is?"¹¹
    - The good name-maker attends to the Form (εἶδος) of the thing named—not the particular hammer but what a hammer is, not this or that shuttle but the Form of Shuttle. Names succeed insofar as they embody in sound the essential nature of what they name, however imperfectly material embodiment can achieve this.
    - This is Logocentric semantics in Platonic formulation: words achieve meaning through participation in Forms, which are themselves the eternal rational structures of being. Convention plays a role—different languages use different sounds—but the sounds are not arbitrarily connected to meanings. Good words are crafted to reflect, within the limits of phonetic material, the essences they name.
    - Chapter Eight: The Sacred Vase—Paradox Without Contradiction
    - How can words be both constructed and sacred? How can they be human artefacts yet vehicles of objective truth? This apparent paradox resolves through what we might call the sacred vase principle.
    - Consider a liturgical vessel. It is manufactured by human craftsmen from earthly materials. In this sense, it is entirely constructed—a product of human labour and decision. Yet when properly consecrated and used, it becomes a sacred vessel—set apart for divine service, participating in mysteries that transcend its material constitution. The construction does not contradict the sacredness; rather, the construction is ordered toward the sacred function. The craftsman shapes the vessel not arbitrarily but according to liturgical requirements. The vessel's form reflects its telos.
    - Words under the Logocentric principle operate similarly. They are human constructions—sounds arranged by convention, evolving through historical usage, varying across languages and dialects. In this sense, constructivism contains a partial truth. But good words are not constructed arbitrarily; they are crafted to receive and transmit objective meaning. The craft involves attending to what the word must carry, shaping the phonetic vessel to hold conceptual content. The word's form reflects its semantic function.
    - The ancients understood this. Greek philosophical vocabulary was deliberately engineered for precision. The pre-Socratics coined terms (ἀρχή, λόγος, φύσις, κόσμος) that preserved in their etymology the essential insights being communicated. Aristotle's technical vocabulary—ἐνέργεια, ἐντελέχεια, οὐσία, δύναμις—was crafted to hold distinctions his thought required. The terms were constructed yet not arbitrary; their construction served correspondence to the realities being investigated.
    - Compare modern neologism. When Ernst Mayr coined "teleonomy" to replace "teleology" in biology, he deliberately constructed a word that appeared Greek while contradicting Greek assumptions. Τέλος (end, purpose) combined with νόμος (law) should mean "the law of ends"—but Mayr intended exactly the opposite: end-directedness without genuine purpose. The vocabulary was designed to smuggle in conclusions while appearing neutral.
    - This is the corruption of sacred vessel: words constructed not to receive objective meaning but to prevent its recognition. The Logocentric principle demands vocabulary crafted to serve truth; the magical principle permits vocabulary crafted to serve power. The former produces sacred vessels; the latter produces counterfeit currency.

    - PART THREE: HAMARTIA AND THE ACCOUNTABILITY OF SPEECH
    - Chapter Nine: Why Sin Becomes Incomprehensible Under Magic
    - The concept of ἁμαρτία (hamartia)—typically translated "sin" though more literally "missing the mark"—presupposes the Logocentric principle. To miss the mark requires that there be a mark to miss. Sin as failure presupposes a standard being failed. If reality exhibits objective rational order, then actions contrary to that order are objectively deficient regardless of human convention or intention. But if "meaning" is human construction, then standards are human projections, and "sin" reduces to violating arbitrary taboos.
    - Aristotle used ἁμαρτία in the Poetics for the tragic hero's error—the miscalculation or flaw that brings catastrophe.¹² The term carries no necessary moral condemnation; it names objective failure against an objective standard. The archer who misses commits hamartia whether or not he intended to hit. The tragic hero who miscalculates commits hamartia whether or not his intentions were noble.
    - This objectivity is precisely what the magical principle cannot accommodate. If meaning is constructed, then standards are constructed, then failure against standards is failure against constructions, then changing constructions changes what counts as failure. "Sin" becomes merely "that which we currently disapprove"—subject to revision as constructions shift. The word retains rhetorical force (useful for condemning opponents) while losing ontological ground.
    - Observe modern discourse about morality. Academic ethics generates elaborate theories—consequentialism, deontology, virtue ethics, care ethics—while increasingly unable to ground any of them.     - PART FOUR: THE ERGONOMIC PRIMING OF CLASSICAL VOCABULARY
    - Chapter Twelve: The Engineering of Greek Philosophical Language
    - Greek philosophical vocabulary was not discovered but engineered. The pre-Socratics and their successors deliberately crafted terms whose etymology would prime the learner toward essential recognitions. This was not pedantry but pedagogy at the deepest level—shaping vocabulary to shape thought to shape soul.
    - Consider πίστις (pistis). Standardly translated "faith" or "belief," the term derives from πείθω (peitho)—"to persuade." The etymology encodes a recognition that modern discourse has systematically obscured: all faith is product of persuasion, all trust emerges through rhetorical process. The Greek learner encountering πίστις is primed to ask: persuaded by what? through what process? toward what end?
    - This priming exposes the naive dichotomy "faith versus reason." If pistis is inherently connected to persuasion, and if persuasion operates through reasoned argument (λόγος), then faith and reason are not opposed but intertwined. Religious faith is persuasion to truth through miracles and testimony; scientific conviction is persuasion to truth through evidence and demonstration. Both are pistis; they differ in the mode and grounds of persuasion, not in being opposed categories.
    - Paul's usage confirms the etymological priming. "Πειθόμενοι εἰς τὴν ἀλήθειαν"—being persuaded toward truth.¹⁷ Faith is not blind assent but response to persuasive evidence. The contrast with modern "blind faith" could not be starker. The Greek vocabulary resists the caricature because its engineering encodes the truth.
    - Consider ἐπιστήμη (episteme). Typically translated "knowledge" or "science," the term combines ἐπί (upon, over) with ἵστημι (to stand). Episteme is that which stands firm, that upon which one can take a stand. The etymology primes the learner toward the stability criterion: real knowledge provides sure footing; opinion leaves one unsettled. The scientist (ἐπιστήμων) is not merely someone who collects facts but someone who has achieved standing ground—stable understanding that supports further investigation.
    - This priming exposes the instability of contemporary science's self-understanding. If episteme requires standing firm, then a "science" that constantly overturns its foundations is not achieving episteme but approaching it—or perhaps moving away. The word itself provides diagnostic criteria: Does this "scientific knowledge" provide stable footing? Can one build upon it? Or does it shift with each paradigm change, leaving practitioners perpetually ungrounded?
    - Consider ἀλήθεια (aletheia). Typically translated "truth," the term is structured as privative: α- (un-) + λήθη (forgetting, concealment). Truth is the un-forgotten, the un-concealed—that which emerges from hiddenness into disclosure. The etymology primes the learner toward recognition that truth requires effort to achieve: the natural state is concealment; truth must be wrested from hiddenness through disciplined inquiry.
    - Heidegger made much of this etymology, perhaps too much.¹⁸ But the basic insight stands: Greek vocabulary encodes that truth is achievement, not default state. The world conceals itself; error is easy; truth requires the labour of unconcealment. This priming resists the modern assumption that truth is obvious or that language naturally reveals rather than conceals.
    - Chapter Thirteen: The Violation of Eidogenesis—Teleonomy as Case Study
    - The principle of eidogenesis names the natural growth of vocabulary from established roots according to the logical structure those roots establish. Greek philosophical vocabulary was eidogenic—new terms emerged organically from existing roots, carrying forward the semantic commitments embedded in those roots. This allowed competing schools to share vocabulary while debating substance.
    - Epicureans and Platonists, Aristotelians and Stoics could all use τέλος (end, purpose, completion) because the word permitted extension in multiple directions. Does telos require consciousness? Can inanimate things have telos? What is the telos of the cosmos? These remained live questions precisely because the vocabulary was neutral between competing answers. The word served investigation rather than prejudging conclusions.
    - Modern neologism systematically violates eidogenesis. Consider "teleonomy," coined by Colin Pittendrigh and popularised by Ernst Mayr to describe biological end-directedness without implying genuine purpose. The word combines τέλος (end) with νόμος (law)—which should mean "the law of ends" or "regulation toward purpose." But Mayr explicitly intended the term to deny that biological ends involve real purpose:
    - "Teleonomy: the apparent purposiveness of living systems... the seeming end-directedness of many organismic processes can be explained without the invocation of vital forces or conscious purposes."¹⁹
    - The neologism is anti-eidogenic. It borrows the prestige of Greek vocabulary while contradicting the semantic commitments that vocabulary encodes. Τέλος in Greek always implied real end-directedness; the formation τελεο-νομία should name genuine regulation toward purpose. Mayr's coinage exploits the etymological resonance while embedding the opposite conclusion.
    - This is proprietary vocabulary capture: designing words to serve one school's conclusions rather than to facilitate open investigation. The investigator who wants to argue that biological systems exhibit genuine (not merely apparent) purpose finds the vocabulary resisting cooperation. "Teleonomy" is engineered to close the question while appearing to address it.
    - The standard rebuttal—"you commit the etymology fallacy"—misses the point entirely. The etymology fallacy applies to natural semantic drift in ordinary language. "Nice" once meant "foolish"; contemporary usage needn't match etymology. But philosophical Greek was not ordinary language. It was engineered vocabulary designed for precision, with etymology serving as mnemonic architecture ensuring derivative words maintain connection to foundational concepts. To dismiss etymological analysis of a deliberately-constructed neologism is to pretend that the construction was innocent—which it manifestly was not.
    - Chapter Fourteen: The Proportionality and Symmetry of Classical Terms
    - Two additional principles governed classical vocabulary formation: proportionality and symmetry.
    - Proportionality means scaling vocabulary to subject matter—being big with the big and small with the small. The Greek term for "being" (τὸ ὄν) has maximal extension; terms for specific beings have proportionally restricted scope. The vocabulary mirrors the ontological hierarchy it names. This primes the learner toward proper intellectual humility: one speaks differently about first principles and particular instances; the vocabulary itself enforces the distinction.
    - Modern discourse routinely violates proportionality. Ultimate terms (being, truth, good, real) are deployed casually for trivial subjects. The vocabulary of first philosophy becomes marketing copy. The effect is systematic degradation: when everything is "authentic," nothing is; when every preference becomes "value," values dissolve.
    - Symmetry means permitting free extension of meaning in the direction the investigation requires. Classical terms were crafted to serve inquiry, not constrain it. Φύσις (nature) could extend toward growth, toward essence, toward cosmic order, toward spontaneous regularity—the term served whichever extension the investigation required. Λόγος could extend toward word, reason, ratio, argument, principle—the term enabled rather than blocked various lines of thought.
    - Proprietary neologism violates symmetry by blocking certain extensions. "Teleonomy" is designed to block extension toward genuine purpose. "Meme" is designed to block extension toward non-mechanistic cultural transmission. "Social construct" is designed to block extension toward objective reality. Each term serves a predetermined conclusion by making alternative conclusions linguistically awkward.
    - The recovery of Logocentric semantics requires recovering these principles. Vocabulary must be evaluated not merely for precision but for proportionality (does it scale properly to its subject?) and symmetry (does it serve inquiry or prejudge conclusions?). Terms that fail these tests—however technically precise—corrupt rather than enable thought.
